## The IELTS Speaking Mistakes 90% of Candidates Make in 2026

You've practised. You've studied vocabulary lists. You've read model answers. So why does your IELTS Speaking score keep falling short of Band 7? The answer, for most candidates, isn't a lack of English — it's a handful of specific IELTS speaking mistakes that examiners see every single day. In 2026, the band descriptors place even more weight on natural, spontaneous communication. If you're still relying on old habits, they're quietly costing you half a band — or more.



## Mistake 1: Memorising Answers Word for Word

This is the single most damaging thing you can do in the Speaking test. You might think a polished, rehearsed answer sounds impressive. To your examiner, it sounds robotic — and they're trained to spot it immediately.

When you recite a memorised script, your fluency drops at the seams, your intonation flattens, and you stop responding to what the examiner actually said. The 2026 band descriptors explicitly penalise responses that feel rehearsed because they fail the core criterion of interactive communication.

What to do instead: Prepare topic frameworks, not scripts. Know the kinds of ideas you'd discuss around common themes (travel, technology, education, health), but let your sentences form in real time. Slight hesitation while you genuinely think is far better than robotic delivery.



## Mistake 2: Giving One-Sentence Answers in Part 1

Part 1 asks simple questions about familiar topics — your hometown, your daily routine, your hobbies. Many candidates treat these as warm-up questions and answer in one or two words. That's a Band 5 response.

Examiners need to hear you speak to assess your fluency, vocabulary, and grammar. A one-sentence answer gives them almost nothing to work with — and you can't score well on criteria that have no evidence.

What to do instead: Use the Point–Reason–Example structure. Answer the question, give a reason, add a specific detail or example. For "Do you enjoy cooking?" — don't say "Yes, I do." Say: "Yes, I really enjoy it — I find it a great way to unwind after a long day. I especially like trying out recipes from different cuisines; last week I made a Moroccan tagine for the first time." That's three times the language evidence with no extra vocabulary required.



### Mistake 3: Ignoring Pronunciation — It's Not About Your Accent

One of the most persistent myths in IELTS preparation is that you need a British or American accent to score well. You don't. What examiners assess under Pronunciation is whether your speech is easy to understand — your use of stress, rhythm, and intonation — not where you grew up.

The real IELTS speaking mistakes here are: swallowing word endings, misplacing stress on multi-syllable words, and speaking in a monotone. These genuinely reduce clarity and pull your Pronunciation band down.

What to do instead: Record yourself answering a Part 2 cue card and play it back. Listen for words you run together, endings you drop, and sentences where your pitch never changes. Even five minutes of shadowing a podcast or news broadcast daily will improve your rhythm noticeably within two weeks.



## Mistake 4: Falling Apart in Part 2 (The Cue Card)

Part 2 gives you one minute to prepare a two-minute talk on a cue card topic. Most candidates either rush through their answer in 60 seconds or freeze after 90. Both outcomes hurt your Fluency and Coherence score significantly.

The pressure of speaking continuously in a second language for two full minutes is real — but it's a skill you can build with deliberate practice.

What to do instead: Use your one minute of preparation time to write three to four bullet points, not full sentences. Structure your talk: opening sentence → bullet 1 → bullet 2 → bullet 3 → closing sentence. Practise this format with a timer three times a week. Within a month, two minutes will feel natural.
