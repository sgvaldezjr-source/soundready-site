# Identifying Key Features in IELTS Task 1: The Complete Checklist

![IELTS Task 1 Feature Identification](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sgvaldezjr-source/soundready-site/main/public/blog-images/2026-06-29-task1-hero-titled.jpg)

> **Key Takeaways**
> - Key features are trends (up/down/flat), patterns, and significant data points — not every number
> - Always read the title, axis labels, and units first to understand what you're analyzing
> - Use a systematic checklist to spot highs, lows, major changes, and exceptions in your data
> - Band 7 responses select and compare features; Band 6 responses list all data
> - Most students underestimate this step — spend 2–3 minutes analyzing before you write

## Introduction

You've got 20 minutes to write an IELTS Academic Task 1 report. Sounds tight, but here's the secret: the first thing you do after reading the prompt isn't writing. It's identifying what actually matters in that graph, chart, or diagram in front of you.

Most candidates rush straight into describing numbers. They mention every data point, every trend, every exception — and their essays end up unfocused, wordy, and stuck at Band 6. Meanwhile, Band 7 writers do something different: they pause, analyze, and identify only the key features the prompt is asking for.

This guide walks you through exactly how to identify those key features, step by step, using a checklist you can apply to every single IELTS Task 1 prompt you encounter.

## Step 1: Read the Prompt Instruction, Not Just the Title

Your prompt has two parts: the visual (graph, chart, table, map) and the task instruction. Most students skip the instruction and go straight to the visual.

**Don't.**

The second sentence tells you what to do. It might say:
- "Summarize the information by selecting and reporting the main features"
- "Describe the data and make comparisons where relevant"
- "Identify the stages in the process"

Pay attention to that exact wording. You're not being asked to interpret *why* data changed (that's opinion). You're being asked to **report what you see** and **compare where the prompt asks for it**.

This distinction matters. A student who ignores the instruction might write a detailed analysis of causes — and get marked down for going off-task, even if the writing is otherwise strong.

> **Pro tip:** Underline or mentally note the task instruction. Refer back to it as you identify key features. It narrows your focus and prevents you from getting lost in the data.

## Step 2: Identify the Visual Type and What It's Measuring

Before you identify *features*, you need to understand what you're looking at.

Look at:
1. **The title** — what's being measured? (e.g., "Annual sales by region", "Population growth 2010–2020")
2. **The axis labels** — what are the units? (e.g., dollars, percentage, thousands of people)
3. **The time period** — is this data from 2015–2020, or monthly, or just a snapshot?
4. **The categories** — how many lines, bars, or segments? (e.g., three regions, five product types)

This takes 30 seconds. It saves you from misreading the data and derails the entire essay.

For example, if you see "Percentage (%)" on the Y-axis, you know you're comparing proportions, not absolute numbers. A rise from 30% to 45% is different from a rise from 30 million to 45 million. Understanding the units keeps your descriptions accurate.

## Step 3: Spot the Obvious Features — The Checklist

Now the visual is in front of you and you understand what it measures. Your next step is finding the key features. Use this checklist:

| Feature type | What to look for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Highest value** | What's the maximum? Which category/time period? | Sales peaked at $5.2M in Q4 2019 |
| **Lowest value** | What's the minimum? Which category/time period? | Enrollment was lowest in 2015 at 3,400 students |
| **Overall trend** | Does the data rise, fall, stay flat, or fluctuate? | Revenue increased steadily from 2018–2022 |
| **Major changes** | Where do you see the biggest jumps or drops? | A sharp decline in March (down 15%) |
| **Comparisons** | Which category is highest? Lowest? Are they similar or very different? | North region outperformed South by 2:1 |
| **Exceptions** | Are there data points that break the pattern? | Oil prices rose overall, but dipped sharply in April |
| **Intersections** | Do any lines cross? When and where? | Female participation surpassed male participation in 2017 |

Go through each row. Mark or note the data points that stand out. You'll end up with 4–6 key observations, not 20.

This is your feature list.

## Step 4: Separate Main Features from Supporting Details

Here's where Band 6 and Band 7 diverge.

**Band 6 approach:** Describe everything. "In 2015, sales were $2.1M. In 2016, they were $2.3M. In 2017, they were $2.8M. In 2018, they were $3.1M."

**Band 7 approach:** Identify the main story, then support it. "Sales rose consistently from $2.1M in 2015 to $3.1M in 2018, with the sharpest growth occurring between 2017 and 2018."

The Band 7 version uses the same data but groups it into a pattern. It shows that you've analyzed and understood what the data is saying.

When you analyze your prompt, ask: **What's the one big thing this data is showing me?** Then identify 2–3 supporting details that back that up.

| Don't do this | Do this instead |
|---|---|
| List every single data point | Identify the trend, then support it with key data points |
| Describe all categories equally | Highlight the highest/lowest categories and major differences |
| Mention every year separately | Group years into periods (e.g., "2015–2017" or "early period vs. late period") |
| Include exceptions without context | Note exceptions only if they break a clear pattern |

## Step 5: Check Against the Task Instruction (Again)

Before you write, go back to that task instruction you underlined in Step 1.

The prompt might say:
- "Summarize **the main features**" — pick 2–3, not 10
- "Make **comparisons** where relevant" — so you need at least one direct comparison between categories or time periods
- "Describe **the data**" — stick to what you see; don't guess causes

Your key-feature list should match what the prompt is asking for. If the instruction says "make comparisons" and you've identified mostly trends but no comparisons, go back and look for them.

This keeps your essay on-task and prevents the "answered the wrong question" trap.

## FAQ: Common Questions About Identifying Features

**Q: How many key features should I identify?**
A: Aim for 3–5. Fewer feels incomplete; more starts to feel like you're listing everything. Look for the features that tell the story of the data.

**Q: What if the data has no clear trend — it's all over the place?**
A: That's the key feature: volatility or fluctuation. Describe that pattern and point to the highest and lowest points. Even chaotic data has a story.

**Q: Do I need to calculate percentages or averages?**
A: No. The prompt will already show you the data in the form you need. Your job is to read and report it, not calculate anything beyond simple comparisons (e.g., "twice as high as").

**Q: What if I identify a feature but the prompt doesn't ask me to comment on it?**
A: You don't have to include it. The instruction is your guide. If it says "describe the trend", focus on trends. You don't need to force in information about exceptions if that's not what's asked for.

**Q: How do I know the difference between a "main feature" and a detail?**
A: Main features are the things that would change the overall message if you removed them. Details are supporting evidence. For example, if sales rose from $2M to $5M, the main feature is "growth of $3M". The detail is "Q1 sales were $2.1M".
