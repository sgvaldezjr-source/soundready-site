Most IELTS candidates believe the path to a higher Speaking score runs through idioms. They memorise lists of expressions — "it's a blessing in disguise", "a double-edged sword", "at the end of the day" — and try to drop them into answers as often as possible. The result is speaking that sounds rehearsed, unnatural, and sometimes grammatically strained. And examiners notice.

The reality is more nuanced — and more useful. Idioms matter, but collocations matter more. Understanding the difference, and knowing when to use each, is one of the fastest ways to raise your Lexical Resource score.

## What Examiners Are Actually Scoring

IELTS Speaking is marked against four equally weighted criteria, each worth 25% of your total band score: Fluency and Coherence, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range and Accuracy, and Pronunciation. Lexical Resource is the vocabulary criterion — and it rewards far more than just idioms.

According to the official band descriptors, a Band 7 speaker uses "a range of vocabulary, including less common and idiomatic items, with some awareness of style and collocation." Notice that idioms are listed alongside collocation and style — not above them. Examiners are looking for evidence that you use vocabulary accurately, flexibly, and with an understanding of how words naturally combine. That last point is where most candidates fall short, not because their vocabulary is small, but because they haven't practised the right kind of vocabulary.

## Collocations — The Vocabulary Skill Examiners Actually Reward

A collocation is a pair or group of words that naturally go together in English. Native speakers use them instinctively; IELTS examiners notice immediately when they're wrong.

Consider the difference between these pairs:

| ✓ Natural collocation | ✗ Common error |
|-----------------------|----------------|
| make a decision | do a decision |
| raise awareness | make an awareness |
| work under pressure | work under a pressure |
| meet a deadline | reach a deadline |
| have a significant impact | do a significant impact |

These errors are not grammar mistakes in the traditional sense — the verb forms are correct. They are collocation errors, and they signal to an examiner that your vocabulary knowledge is limited. Conversely, using these phrases accurately signals the kind of natural fluency that separates a Band 6.5 from a Band 7.

Collocations are also far easier to deploy naturally than idioms. They slot into almost any answer about work, study, technology, the environment, or health — the exact topics that appear most frequently in Part 3 of the Speaking test.

> 💡 **Pro tip:** When you learn a new vocabulary item, always learn it in a full phrase rather than in isolation. Instead of learning "decision", learn "make a decision", "reach a decision", and "a difficult decision to make". This triples the usable language you get from every item you study.

## The Right Way to Use Idioms in IELTS Speaking

Idioms are not the enemy — forced idioms are. There is a real difference between an idiom that arises naturally in conversation and one that is inserted because a candidate is trying to impress the examiner.

Compare these two responses to a Part 3 question about social media:

**Forced:** *"Social media is a double-edged sword, and it's a blessing in disguise for young people, but at the end of the day, it's here to stay."*

**Natural:** *"I think social media has genuinely changed how people connect with each other — it's been a double-edged sword in that sense. The benefits are obvious, but so are the problems."*

In the first example, three idioms are stacked in a single sentence. None of them add meaning — they replace it. The examiner's job is to assess vocabulary, and when every idea is expressed through a memorised phrase, there is no evidence of real lexical range.

In the second example, one idiom appears in a context where it actually fits. The candidate explains what they mean before and after using it, which demonstrates understanding rather than memorisation.

The practical rule: aim for one, possibly two idioms across your entire Speaking test. Choose them carefully. Use them in contexts where they genuinely express your meaning — not as decoration.

## How to Build the Right Vocabulary Before Your Test

The most effective IELTS Speaking vocabulary preparation is organised by topic rather than by expression type. Instead of memorising a list of twenty idioms, prepare collocations and topic-specific vocabulary for the themes most likely to appear in Part 2 and Part 3: technology, the environment, health and wellbeing, education, work, and urban life.

For each topic, prepare:
- Five to eight strong collocations (e.g. for technology: *adopt new technology, have a significant impact on daily life, raise privacy concerns, rely heavily on devices*)
- Two or three higher-register adjectives or verbs (e.g. *transformative, unprecedented, accelerate*)
- One idiom that fits naturally within the theme (e.g. for technology: *a double-edged sword*)

This approach means that whatever topic comes up, you have a bank of accurate, flexible vocabulary to draw from — rather than a list of expressions you're trying to force into the answer.

> 💡 **Pro tip:** After preparing vocabulary for each topic, practise using it in a short spoken answer. Record yourself and listen back. If an expression sounds unnatural even to you, it will sound unnatural to an examiner. Cut it and replace it with something simpler that you can deliver confidently.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do idioms really help your IELTS Speaking score?

Yes — but only when used naturally. The IELTS Band 7 descriptor specifically mentions "idiomatic items", so examiners are looking for evidence that you can use idioms. The key word is *items*, plural but not excessive. One or two well-placed idioms demonstrate range without sounding rehearsed. Overusing them — or using them in contexts where they don't fit — signals memorisation, which works against you.

### What is the difference between an idiom and a collocation?

An idiom is an expression whose meaning cannot be deduced from the individual words (e.g. "a blessing in disguise" does not literally mean something hidden is a blessing). A collocation is a pair or group of words that habitually go together in natural English (e.g. "make a decision" rather than "do a decision"). Both matter for IELTS Lexical Resource, but collocations are more numerous, more versatile, and easier to use accurately under exam pressure.

### How many vocabulary items should I prepare for IELTS Speaking?

Quality matters far more than quantity. Preparing five to eight strong collocations per major topic — technology, environment, health, education, work — gives you approximately thirty to forty highly usable phrases. That is more than enough for a Band 7 performance, provided you can deploy them accurately and naturally. A candidate who uses twenty collocations correctly will always outscore one who attempts fifty idioms with mixed results.

### Can I prepare set phrases and use them in the test?

You can prepare vocabulary and expressions in advance — and you should. The distinction is between preparing flexible building blocks (collocations, topic vocabulary, one or two idioms per theme) and memorising complete sentences to recite. Examiners are trained to recognise scripted answers, and a response that sounds pre-prepared does not demonstrate the spontaneous fluency that higher band scores require. Prepare the words, not the sentences.

### What topics should I focus on for IELTS Speaking vocabulary?

The most frequently recurring Part 3 topics are: technology and social media, the environment and climate change, health and wellbeing, education and learning, work and careers, cities and urban life, and culture and tradition. Preparing specific collocations and one strong idiom for each of these seven areas covers the vast majority of Speaking tests.
