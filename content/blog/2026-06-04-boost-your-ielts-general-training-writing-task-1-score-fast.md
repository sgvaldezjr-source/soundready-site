Writing Task 1 is one of the most underestimated sections of the IELTS General Training test — and for visa applicants, it can be the difference between meeting your band requirement and having to resit. Unlike the Academic version, you are not describing a graph. You are writing a letter: formal, semi-formal, or informal, depending on the prompt. That might sound easy, but examiners score your response against four strict criteria, and many candidates lose bands on avoidable mistakes. The practical IELTS General Training Writing Task 1 tips in this guide will help you understand exactly what the examiner expects and how to deliver it every time.

**Understand the Four Marking Criteria First**

Before you write a single word, you need to know how you are being scored. Every General Training letter is marked on the same four criteria, each worth 25% of your Task 1 score:

\- **Task Achievement** — Did you cover all three bullet points fully? Did you write at least 150 words?

\- **Coherence and Cohesion** — Is your letter easy to follow? Do your ideas connect logically?

\- **Lexical Resource** — Are you using a range of vocabulary accurately, including topic-specific words?

\- **Grammatical Range and Accuracy** — Are you using a mix of sentence structures without consistent errors?

Most candidates focus almost entirely on vocabulary and grammar while neglecting Task Achievement. If you address only two of the three bullet points — even with perfect grammar — you will be capped at Band 5 for that criterion. Always read the prompt twice and underline each bullet point before you begin writing.



**Match Your Tone to the Prompt Every Time**

One of the most common IELTS letter writing mistakes is misjudging the tone. Getting this wrong can cost you a full band in both Lexical Resource and Task Achievement.

Here is how to identify the correct register in under 30 seconds:

\- **Formal** — the prompt addresses "a manager", "a landlord", or "an organisation." Use Dear Sir/Madam, no contractions, and sign off Yours faithfully.

\- **Semi-formal** — the prompt addresses "your employer", "your doctor", or "a neighbour you know." Use Dear Mr/Mrs \[Name], a professional but warm tone, and Yours sincerely.

\- **Informal** — the prompt addresses "a friend" or "a family member." Use Dear \[First name], natural contractions, and a casual close like Best wishes.

A simple rule: if the word "friend" appears in the prompt, the letter is informal. Practise writing one letter of each type every week and your tone instincts will sharpen quickly.



**Structure Your Letter to Cover Every Bullet Point**

A strong letter follows a reliable four-paragraph structure that ensures you never miss a bullet point and always reach the word count.

**Paragraph 1** — Opening statement: State your reason for writing in one or two sentences. Paraphrase the task prompt — do not copy it word for word.

**Paragraphs 2–4** — One paragraph per bullet point: Each bullet in the prompt deserves its own paragraph. This makes your letter easy to follow, which directly protects your Coherence and Cohesion score, and guarantees you cover all required content.

**Final paragraph** — Closing statement: End with a forward-looking sentence: "I look forward to hearing from you" (formal) or "Hope to hear from you soon" (informal). Then add your sign-off.

Aim for 160–180 words. Going a little over 150 gives you a buffer, but writing 250 words does not earn extra marks — quality always beats length.



**Boost Your IELTS General Training Writing Task 1 Score with Targeted Practice**

Reading about technique only takes you so far. The candidates who improve fastest practise deliberately and review their writing against the marking criteria. Here is a routine that works:

1. Set a 20-minute timer. In the real exam, Task 1 should take no more than 20 minutes so you have at least 40 minutes for the higher-weighted Task 2.

2. Write from a real prompt. Use official Cambridge or IDP practice materials. Generic letter-writing sites often use vocabulary and conventions that do not match IELTS expectations.

3. Self-check against the four criteria. After writing, ask: Did I cover all bullet points? Is my tone consistent throughout? Have I used varied vocabulary? Are there recurring grammar errors?

4. Get expert feedback. Self-correction has limits. A trained IELTS tutor can identify the specific patterns holding your score back — whether that is overusing simple sentence structures, repeating the same linking phrases, or misreading tone cues.

For migration applicants working towards an IELTS band 6 writing requirement, this kind of structured, feedback-driven practice is what separates candidates who hit their target on the first attempt from those who resit multiple times.
