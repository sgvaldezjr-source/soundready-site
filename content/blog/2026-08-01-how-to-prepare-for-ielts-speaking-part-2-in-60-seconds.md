The examiner slides a cue card across the table. You have one minute — not two, not five — to turn an unfamiliar topic into two minutes of fluent, organized speech. For most candidates, that single minute is the most stressful part of the entire IELTS Speaking test.

If you've ever frozen staring at a blank notepad, or started strong and run out of things to say after 40 seconds, the problem usually isn't your English. It's how you're using your prep time. With the right note-taking method, 60 seconds is more than enough to build a structure you can speak from confidently for the full two minutes.

## Understand What the 60 Seconds Are Actually For

Your prep minute has one job: giving you a skeleton, not a script. Examiners can tell within seconds when a candidate is reciting memorized sentences, and it costs you on Fluency and Coherence. You don't have time to write full sentences anyway — and you don't need to.

Instead, use your minute to answer four questions:

- **What** is the topic, exactly? (Re-read the card once, fully.)
- **Which** of the 3–4 bullet points will you use as your outline?
- **When/where/who** — the concrete details that make your answer specific instead of generic?
- **How** do you feel about it, or why does it matter? (Most cue cards ask for this implicitly.)

You're not planning what to say. You're planning what order to say it in.

## Split Your Minute Into Three Blocks

A minute feels short until you break it into stages. Try this split:

**0–10 seconds — Pick your idea.** Don't search for the "perfect" answer. The first real example that comes to mind is almost always good enough. A believable, ordinary story beats an impressive one you have to invent details for.

**10–45 seconds — Build your outline from the bullet points.** The cue card already gives you free structure — use it. Next to each bullet point, jot two or three keywords, not sentences. For a "describe a skill you learned" card, that might look like:

- What: cooking, learned from grandmother
- When: age 12, summer holidays
- How: watched, then tried, burned a lot of rice
- Why it matters: now cook for family every week

**45–60 seconds — Plan your opening line.** Have your first sentence ready to go so you start with confidence instead of a hesitant "um." Something as simple as "I'd like to talk about a skill I learned from my grandmother when I was around twelve" buys you a smooth, fluent start while your brain catches up with the rest of the outline.

## Use Keywords, Never Full Sentences

Writing full sentences during prep does two things wrong: it eats your 60 seconds, and it tempts you to read from the card instead of speaking naturally, which flattens your intonation and hurts your score. Two or three words per point is the ceiling. Trust that you can build a sentence around a keyword in real time — that's a skill worth practicing before test day, not during it.

## Give Yourself Something to Say for the Full Two Minutes

Running out of things to say around the 60–90 second mark is the single most common Part 2 problem, and it almost always traces back to prep. If your notes only cover the surface-level bullet points, you'll finish early. Build in extra material during your minute by adding:

- One specific sensory or emotional detail (what it looked, sounded, or felt like)
- One comparison ("unlike my sister, I actually enjoyed it")
- One consequence or result ("since then, I've cooked dinner most Sundays")

These extras aren't padding — they're exactly the kind of detail that pushes an answer from "adequate" to genuinely fluent and coherent, because they show you can develop an idea, not just list facts.

## Practice the Skill, Not Just the Topics

Most candidates spend their prep time memorizing answers to likely topics. That's a trap — you can't predict the exact card you'll get, and a memorized answer that doesn't quite fit still sounds memorized. What actually transfers to test day is the 60-second process itself: split the minute, build keyword notes from the bullet points, plan your opener. Practice that process on ten different random cue cards and it becomes automatic, regardless of which topic you draw.
