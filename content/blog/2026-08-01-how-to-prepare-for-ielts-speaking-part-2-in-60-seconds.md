> **Key Takeaways**
> - You get exactly **60 seconds** to prepare for the Part 2 long turn — use it to build a skeleton, not a script.
> - Split the minute into **three blocks**: pick your idea, build keyword notes, plan your opening line.
> - Use the **Response + Reason + Example** formula on each point so you never run dry before two minutes are up.
> - Full sentences during prep cost you time **and** score — two or three keywords per point is the ceiling.
> - Practicing the *process* on random cue cards transfers to test day far better than memorizing topic answers.

The examiner slides a cue card across the table. You have one minute — not two, not five — to turn an unfamiliar topic into two minutes of fluent, organized speech. For most candidates, that single minute is the most stressful part of the entire IELTS Speaking test.

If you've ever frozen staring at a blank notepad, or started strong and run out of things to say after 40 seconds, the problem usually isn't your English. It's how you're using your prep time. With the right note-taking method, 60 seconds is more than enough to build a structure you can speak from confidently for the full two minutes.

## What the 60 Seconds Are Actually For

Your prep minute has one job: giving you a skeleton, not a script. Examiners can tell within seconds when a candidate is reciting memorized sentences, and it costs you on Fluency and Coherence. You don't have time to write full sentences anyway — and you don't need to.

Instead, use your minute to answer four questions:

1. **What** is the topic, exactly? Re-read the card once, fully.
2. **Which** of the 3–4 bullet points will you use as your outline?
3. **When/where/who** — what concrete details make your answer specific instead of generic?
4. **How** do you feel about it, or why does it matter? Most cue cards ask for this implicitly.

You're not planning what to say. You're planning what order to say it in.

## The 3-Block Prep Method

A minute feels short until you break it into stages.

**Block 1 (0–10 seconds) — Pick your idea.** Don't search for the "perfect" answer. The first real example that comes to mind is almost always good enough. A believable, ordinary story beats an impressive one you have to invent details for.

**Block 2 (10–45 seconds) — Build your outline from the bullet points.** The cue card already gives you free structure — use it. Next to each bullet point, jot two or three keywords, not sentences. For a "describe a skill you learned" card, that might look like:

- What: cooking, learned from grandmother
- When: age 12, summer holidays
- How: watched, then tried, burned a lot of rice
- Why it matters: now cook for family every week

**Block 3 (45–60 seconds) — Plan your opening line.** Have your first sentence ready to go so you start with confidence instead of a hesitant "um." Something as simple as "I'd like to talk about a skill I learned from my grandmother when I was around twelve" buys you a smooth, fluent start while your brain catches up with the rest of the outline.

> **Pro tip:** Practice the 3-block split with a phone timer before test day. Most candidates who "run out of time" in the real exam have never actually timed themselves in practice — the minute feels completely different once you've drilled it.

## Use the Response + Reason + Example Formula

Running out of things to say around the 60–90 second mark is the single most common Part 2 problem, and it almost always traces back to prep. The fix is a simple three-part formula you can apply to every bullet point on the card: **Response, Reason, Example.**

- **Response** — Answer the point directly. State the fact.
- **Reason** — Explain why. What caused it, or why does it matter to you?
- **Example/Detail** — Ground it with something concrete: a moment, a number, a name, a sensory detail.

Applied to "describe a skill you learned," one point might expand like this:

- *Response:* "I taught myself to cook."
- *Reason:* "because my grandmother was too busy running her market stall in the summers to cook for the whole family."
- *Example/Detail:* "I still remember burning an entire pot of rice on my first attempt — by the end of that summer I could finally get it right."

Run each of your two or three keyword points through this formula during your prep minute, and you'll never run dry at 90 seconds. It also does double duty for your score: reasoning and concrete examples are exactly what separate a Band 7 answer from a Band 5 list of facts, so the formula fills your time and lifts your Fluency & Coherence at the same time.

## Do This, Not That: Common Prep Mistakes

| Weak Prep Habit | Strong Prep Habit |
|---|---|
| Writing full sentences during your minute | Writing 2–3 keywords per bullet point |
| Memorizing model answers for likely topics | Practicing the 3-block *process* on random cards |
| Starting with a hesitant "um" while you think | Planning your opening line in Block 3 |
| Listing bullet points with no development | Expanding each point with Response + Reason + Example |
| Panicking if the topic feels unfamiliar | Picking the first believable idea and moving on |

## FAQ

**What if I can't think of an idea in the first 10 seconds?**
Don't wait for the perfect answer — pick the first real, believable idea and move straight to Block 2. You can always add detail later with the Response + Reason + Example formula; a plain idea with strong development beats an impressive idea with none.

**Do I have to cover every bullet point on the card?**
No. The bullet points are a guide, not a checklist the examiner ticks off. It's fine to spend more time on two points you can develop well than to rush through all four superficially.

**What happens if I finish before the two minutes are up?**
The examiner will simply ask a follow-up question. It's not a penalty, but finishing well before 90 seconds is often a sign your notes needed more development — the Response + Reason + Example formula helps prevent this.

**Can I ask the examiner to change the topic?**
No — the cue card is fixed. If the topic feels unfamiliar, adapt it: for "describe a book you enjoyed," you can talk about a book you merely finished, or reframe the story slightly. Examiners assess your language, not the literal truth of your anecdote.

**Should I write full sentences if I finish my notes early?**
No. Use any leftover seconds to add one more Response + Reason + Example point, or to firm up your opening line. Extra notes rarely help — extra structure does.
